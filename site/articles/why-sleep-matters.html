A deep dive into sleep — the most underrated power hack in your career and life

Explore what really happens in your brain while you sleep and why every stage matters

    You know that feeling on a Monday morning when you're standing in the kitchen, staring at the coffee machine and your body is technically standing vertical, but something seems wrong?

    We often search for the next productivity breakthrough: a better morning routine, another cup of coffee, a new app, a smarter workflow. Yet the most powerful performance enhancer has been with us all along.

    The most revolutionary thing you can do for your career might just be to go to bed.

    Why do we even sleep?

    Sleep has no benefits? If that were true, nature wouldn't plunge us into this useless state every single night. Consider that question seriously for a moment - you spend roughly 30% of your entire life asleep. If that time served no function, evolution would have ruthlessly removed it long ago. Sleep is also not a passive state. It is, in many ways, where the most essential life happens. When your eyes close and your body stills, your brain enters one of the most complex and carefully orchestrated biological sequences we know of.

    Here is what is actually happening while you sleep

    Your brain moves through repeating 90-minute cycles, each containing distinct stages. Each stage has a different biological signature and a different job to do. A full night of sleep contains 4 to 5 of these complete cycles. Miss the early ones and you lose your deepest physical repair: growth hormone is released, tissues rebuild, the immune system works. Cut the night short before the later cycles and you strip away the REM-heavy phases that are essential for emotional processing, creative synthesis and memory consolidation. All phases matter - they cannot substitute for each other.

    Let’s dive into the each cycle. The cycle begins with the first stage— the threshold between wakefulness and sleep, lasting just 5 to 10 minutes, where you may feel a sudden jerk or a sensation of falling. Second stage follows: deeper, longer, around 20 minutes. Body temperature drops, heart rate and digestion slows down. The brain quietly files the day's work. Stage 3 is the deepest station — slow-wave sleep: muscles fully relax, blood pressure and breathing slow, and the body becomes nearly unreachable by external stimulation. Then comes REM sleep — the paradoxical stage, so named because the brain's electrical activity during it looks almost identical to full wakefulness: eyes dart rapidly beneath closed lids, breathing becomes irregular and heart rate rises. It lasts just 10 to 20 minutes in the first cycle but grows substantially with each cycle, which is why the final hours of a full night's sleep are so rich in dreaming and emotional processing. Cut those hours and you cut the very cognitive work that makes you most human. Crucially, the sequence might not be linear. Sleep does not move neatly from first stage through to REM and stop. After deep slow-wave sleep, the brain typically ascends back through second stage before entering REM. A healthy night is defined by the rhythmicity of this cycling, not simply by total hours, which is why fragmented sleep, even of technically sufficient duration, leaves you feeling as though you barely rested at all.

    Why sleep matters for your brain and performance

    Sleep is not time lost. The quality of your life is shaped long before your morning alarm rings. 

    So before searching for the next productivity hack, ask yourself a simpler question: have you given your brain the chance to do its most important work?

    Because success isn't built only in the hours you're awake – it is built while you're asleep.
