The Chemistry Behind a Good Night's Sleep

Have you ever wondered why some evenings you can barely keep your eyes open, while on others you're lying in bed wide awake, even though you're exhausted?

Let's talk about what's actually happening inside you— the invisible chemical ballet that either carries you gently into rest or keeps you staring at the ceiling calculating how many hours you have left if you fall asleep right now.

Melatonin is perhaps the most misunderstood molecule in the sleep conversation. It is not a sedative. It is a timing signal, one of the most ancient biological signals on Earth. The rhythm is precise: secretion begins around 9–10pm, peaks between 2am and 4am and falls to near zero by 7–9am in the morning.

Cortisol operates as melatonin's hormonal counterpart — they move in near-perfect opposition across the 24-hour cycle. As melatonin rises through the evening, cortisol falls to its daily low. Then, around 6am, as melatonin fades, cortisol rises, peaking by 7am to drive morning alertness, focus and physical readiness. This is your body's internal alarm clock, and it is far more sophisticated than anything on your phone. A healthy cortisol curve produces the sensation of waking up and gradually becoming more alive. A disrupted one, flattened by chronic stress, irregular sleep, or alcohol, means waking already wired, already anxious, or conversely, waking flat and foggy and reaching for coffee before you've even stood up.

But not everyone experiences this rhythm in exactly the same way.

Your sleep identity - the chronotypes

You have probably always known you have a sleep type – your chronotype. You've said it yourself — I'm not a morning person or I come alive after dark. What nobody told you is that this isn't a preference, a character trait or a discipline. It is genetic. It is written into your DNA - encoded in specific gene variants that researchers have now identified with considerable precision. The key players include variants in the Period 1 gene (rs7221412), the CLOCK gene (rs1801260),and the PER3 gene (rs228697) — the same molecular clock genes that regulate your 24-hour biological rhythm at the cellular level.

There is also an important distinction between genetic chronotype and behavioural chronotype. Your genetic wiring sets a strong tendency, but behaviour, light exposure, work schedules and social habits can shift your expressed chronotype by several hours — sometimes significantly.

Key takeaway

The timing of your sleep is not determined by willpower alone. It is carefully regulated by hormones and genes that have evolved over millions of years.

When your internal clock is in sync, falling asleep becomes easier, waking feels more natural, and every system in your body performs more efficiently.

The better you understand your biology, the better you can work with it rather than against it.
